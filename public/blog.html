How to Feed 10, 50, or 100 People in Regina (Without Losing Your Mind)

📅 Published September 2026 · ⏱️ 7 min read · 🍽️ Catering

You got handed the job. Somebody has to organize food for the office lunch, the retirement party, the job site, the wedding social — and that somebody is you. Here's everything we've learned feeding groups across Regina, including the mistakes that cost people money and the math that actually works.

Start with the only number that matters: how many are eating?

Not how many were invited. Not how many RSVP'd. How many will actually put food on a plate.

For an office lunch, assume nearly everyone who's in the building that day eats — free food at work has close to a 100% take rate. For an evening party, expect 10–20% of your RSVPs not to show. For a job site, count the crew and add two, because someone always brings a buddy.

Order for the realistic number, not the optimistic one. Running out is a disaster people remember. Having a little extra is a minor cost nobody notices.

The portion math nobody tells you

Here's what actually works, based on what we send out of our kitchens every week:

  • Wings as the main event, no sides: 10–12 wings per person. Hungry crews and evening events push toward 12.
  • Wings with appetizers and sides: 7–8 wings per person. This is the sweet spot for most office lunches.
  • Wings as one option among several mains: 5–6 wings per person.
  • Appetizer trays: One tray feeds roughly 12 people as a shared side. Two trays for 25, five or six for 70.
  • Salad: One large bowl per 10 people. Always order it even if you think nobody wants salad — it disappears faster than people predict, and it's the only thing some guests will eat.
  • Drinks: One and a half per person for a lunch, two or more for an evening event.

The single most common mistake we see: ordering wings for exactly the headcount with no sides. Wings alone go fast, and you end up with people circling an empty tray twenty minutes in.

Mix your heat levels or you'll strand somebody

This is the part people underthink. If you order everything in one flavour, you've made a decision for thirty other people — and someone in that room can't eat it.

The reliable formula for a mixed group of any size:

  • One sweet — Honey Garlic or BBQ. Safe for everybody, including kids.
  • One mild — Garlic Parmesan or Lemon Pepper. For people who want flavour without heat.
  • One medium — Buffalo or Prairie Heat. The default crowd-pleaser.
  • One hot — Devil's Kiss or Dead Angel. Somebody in every group wants to prove something.

With 135+ flavours you can get more adventurous than that, but if you only remember one thing: cover all four heat levels and nobody goes home hungry. We pack every flavour in its own labelled tray so nothing bleeds together and people can actually tell what they're grabbing.

Bone-in or boneless? It depends on the room

Bone-in is the real thing — better flavour, better texture, and it's what wings are supposed to be. But it's messy, and mess matters depending on where people are eating.

Go boneless when: people are eating at their desks, wearing work clothes they can't get sauce on, standing up at a mingling event, or eating one-handed while doing something else.

Go bone-in when: people are sitting at tables with napkins, it's an evening or weekend event, or the crowd genuinely cares about wings.

Go half and half when you don't know the room. It's the safest answer for a mixed office, and nobody has ever complained about having a choice.

Timing: the part that goes wrong most often

Give your caterer at least 48 hours. For groups over 75, three to four days is better — not because we can't move fast, but because advance notice is what guarantees you get the flavours you asked for instead of whatever's prepped.

Then work backwards from when people eat, not when the food arrives:

  • Pickup: Add 15 minutes of buffer for parking and loading. Wing trays are bulkier than people expect — bring somebody to help, or ask about curbside.
  • Delivery: Schedule it 20–30 minutes before you want people eating. That gives you time to set up, and it means the food isn't sitting out losing heat.
  • Setup: Whoever's organizing should be free for 15 minutes when the food lands. Don't schedule yourself into a meeting that ends at noon for a noon lunch.

Ask about dietary needs before you order, not after

Send one message to the group: "Any allergies or dietary restrictions I should know about?" It takes thirty seconds and saves the situation where one person stands there with nothing to eat.

For vegetarians, cauliflower wings tossed in any of our flavours genuinely work — most people don't clock the difference until they look closely. Perogies, mozza sticks, poppers and fries trays are all meat-free too. For anything more serious, tell your caterer directly. Our kitchen handles gluten, dairy, nuts and shellfish in shared space, and we'd rather be straight with you about that upfront than have someone find out the hard way.

What catering actually costs in Regina

Per-person is the number to compare, not the total. A quote that sounds expensive for 60 people might be cheaper per head than a smaller order somewhere else.

Bundles almost always beat ordering the same items individually, because the kitchen can batch the prep. The bigger your group, the better the per-person rate gets — that's true just about everywhere, so if you're near a threshold, ask what happens if you round up.

Watch for the extras that don't show in the headline price: delivery fees, setup charges, service fees, and whether plates and napkins are included or billed separately. Ours includes plates, napkins and serving tongs, and delivery is a flat rate anywhere in Regina.

Five mistakes we see constantly

  • Ordering too late. Same-day catering means you take what's available, not what you want.
  • One flavour for everybody. Guarantees somebody can't eat.
  • No sides. Wings alone vanish and the table looks empty fast.
  • Forgetting drinks. Especially with spicy flavours — people will hunt for something to drink.
  • No plan for leftovers. Bring containers. People will take food home, and it saves you throwing out good wings.

📖 Wing Wisdom

How to Eat Wings Like a Pro (The Sauce Boss Method)

📅 Published 2026 · ⏱️ 3 min read · 🍗 Hot Take

There's a right way and a wrong way to eat wings. Most people are doing it wrong. Here's how to fix that.

The 4 rules of wing eating

  • Rule 1: Drumstick first. Easier to eat, more meat, no learning curve.
  • Rule 2: Two-bite rule for flats. Pull out the two bones, eat the meat clean.
  • Rule 3: Don't waste sauce. Use a fry to scoop residual sauce off the plate. That's where flavor lives.
  • Rule 4: Pace yourself with heat. Start mild, work up. Reverse order ruins your taste buds.

The tools matter

Forks are for amateurs. Wings are a hands experience. Have wet wipes ready, embrace the mess, and pair with the right drink (cold beer for buffalo, milk for Reaper-level heat).
